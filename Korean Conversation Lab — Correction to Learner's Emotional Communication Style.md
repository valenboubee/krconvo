## Correction: Learner's Emotional Communication Style

### Core correction

Do **not** portray the learner as someone who gives direct instructions, commands, or rigid advice.

The learner generally does **not** say things like:

- "You should do this."
- "You shouldn't do that."
- "You have to do this."
- "You need to stop doing that."
- "Just do X."

That style is too rigid and does not accurately represent the learner's personality.

Instead, the learner tends to respond through:

1. acknowledging and normalizing the other person's feelings
2. offering a different way of looking at the situation
3. explaining why the difficult feeling is understandable
4. gently pointing toward a more hopeful interpretation
5. encouraging the person without telling them what they must do

The tone should feel:

- warm
- patient
- thoughtful
- reassuring
- empathetic
- non-judgmental
- encouraging
- conversational

The learner is more likely to **help someone see something differently** than to **tell them what to do**.

---

## The learner's typical pattern

A common structure is:

> "It's understandable that you feel X. At the same time, Y is also true. It might be difficult now, but Z often gets easier with time/practice."

For example, instead of:

> "You should be more confident."

the learner's style is closer to:

> "It's normal to feel nervous when you're doing something new. But I think it's really great that you're willing to try it. The difficult part might take some time, but once you get through it, it'll probably feel much easier. And the more you do it, the more confident you'll become."

The important feature is **gentle reframing**, not instruction.

---

## Emotional-support language to prioritize

When writing dialogue for the learner, prioritize conversational intentions such as:

### Normalizing

> "It's normal to feel that way."

> "I think anyone would feel nervous in that situation."

> "That makes sense."

### Reframing

> "Maybe it's not necessarily a bad thing."

> "I think you're looking at it from one side right now."

> "There might be another way to look at it."

> "I don't think that means you did badly."

### Encouraging

> "I think it's really good that you tried."

> "Even if it was difficult, you still did it."

> "The first time is probably the hardest."

> "Once you're used to it, I think it'll feel much easier."

### Gentle perspective

> "I think you're probably being a little hard on yourself."

> "Maybe it feels bigger because it's still new."

> "The more you do it, the more comfortable you'll probably feel."

### Emotional reassurance

> "You don't have to have everything figured out right now."

> "It's okay if you're still figuring it out."

> "I think you're doing better than you think."

These should be translated into natural Korean appropriate to the learner's level rather than copied literally.

---

## Avoid directive advice

When authoring learner dialogue, flag or rewrite lines that rely heavily on:

- 해야 돼
- 해야 해
- 하지 마
- ~하는 게 좋아
- ~해야 할 것 같아

when they are being used as blunt instructions.

These forms are not forbidden.

They are perfectly legitimate Korean.

The problem is **overusing directive constructions to represent the learner's personality**.

A learner character who repeatedly says things equivalent to:

> "You should..."

> "You need to..."

> "You have to..."

will feel unlike the intended speaker.

When expressing the learner's personality, prefer structures that allow softer reflection, such as concepts equivalent to:

- "I think..."
- "Maybe..."
- "I feel like..."
- "It's understandable..."
- "It might..."
- "It seems..."
- "I don't think..."
- "I think it's natural..."
- "Once..."
- "The more..., the more..."
- "Even if..."
- "Rather than..."
- "Maybe it's because..."

The exact Korean grammar should vary according to the learner's current level and the target structure of the lesson.

---

## Important distinction: support is not problem-solving

The learner does not automatically try to solve the other person's problem.

A conversation may end with:

> "Yeah, I understand."

> "That makes sense."

> "I think it'll get easier."

> "You did well."

without giving any concrete solution.

This is important because emotional support itself is part of the learner's real conversational needs.

---

## Preferred example

Situation:

The girlfriend is nervous about doing something unfamiliar.

### Do NOT write:

> "You should just do it. Once you do it, you'll get used to it."

### Prefer:

> "It's normal to feel nervous when you're doing something new. But I think it's great that you're willing to try. The difficult part will probably feel easier once you've done it a few times. And the more you do it, the more confident you'll feel."

The Korean version should preserve this **emotional logic**, not merely translate the English words.

---

## Apply this throughout the curriculum

Whenever a conversation involves:

- stress
- anxiety
- insecurity
- career decisions
- studying
- difficult experiences
- mistakes
- relationships
- confidence
- uncertainty
- personal growth
- trying something new

the learner should generally respond as:

> **validate → reframe → encourage**

rather than:

> **diagnose → instruct → prescribe**

This distinction should be treated as a standing content-authoring rule for all future conversations.
